Challenges and Support Strategies in Online Piano Learning: A Content Analysis of Online Discussion Forums

Liujin Chen · School of Music, College of Design, Art & Performance · University of South Florida

NAfME 2026 · poster presentation

The poster’s content is summarised as text in the “At a glance” section below.

At a glance

the poster, condensed

Purpose

To identify and categorize the common challenges and needs of online piano learners and describe the support strategies offered by the community.

Research questions

  1. What difficulties do online piano learners report?
  2. What strategies and support does the community offer?
  3. Which strategies correspond to which difficulties?
  4. To what extent do a researcher and a LLM agree in screening the data using the same criteria?

Who answered

  1. Fellow learners: from beginners a few weeks in to long-time hobbyists.
  2. Teachers: often citing their years of teaching.
  3. Commercial promoters: own courses, channels, or “DM me”; some were teachers.

Themes

  1. Competing options, missing criteria — Learners faced many competing courses, apps, and videos, but had no clear way to decide on them. Independent reviews were scarce, so they asked peers for first-hand experience.
  2. Online lesson as fallback — About a quarter of the learners treated online lessons as a second choice. Replies seldom argued that online was better; they said it was not ideal, but good enough to start.
  3. Rebuilding seeing and hearing at a distance — When live online lessons came up, set-up talk centred on what the teacher could see and hear: hands, posture, feet on the pedals, and mistakes.
  4. From feeling to tasks — When learners expressed frustration, boredom, or overwhelm, replies rarely stayed with the feeling. They first framed it as normal, then turned it into a task: how to practice, or what to do about the teacher.

Conclusions

  1. Online piano learners struggled most with choosing: what to use, from whom to learn, and whether online learning could work at all. The problem was not a lack of resources but a lack of criteria to judge them.
  2. The community responded mainly by pointing to resources and sharing first-hand experience. Much of the advice described options rather than telling learners what to do next.
  3. Resource recommendations were given regardless of the difficulty type. Only a few difficulties drew matching help: set-up problems received set-up advice, and feelings were addressed with tasks.
  4. The LLM screened the data much like the researcher, with a high degree of similarity. Disagreements arose when the screening criteria required interpretation.
